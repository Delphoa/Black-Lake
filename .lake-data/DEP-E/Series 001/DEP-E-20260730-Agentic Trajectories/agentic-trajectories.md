## Executive Summary

The selected DEP brings together ten late-June 2026 papers that initially appear heterogeneous. Their strongest common thread is methodological: consequential systems should be evaluated as trajectories through an environment, not as isolated model responses. The trajectory includes the harness, tools, information routing, executable feedback, intermediate state, acceptance rule, and human or automated review.

The adoption evidence in S3 shows why this unit matters operationally. The authors report more than fivefold active-user growth during the first half of 2026; more than 10% of users managed at least three concurrent Codex agents during a week; and skill use rose from 5.4% to 26.6% between March 1 and June 11. These are platform observations rather than causal productivity estimates, but they show delegation becoming longer, parallel, and codified.

The strongest evaluation warning comes from S4-S7. scBench-Long's best model–harness pair passed only 16 of 63 runs, and the same base model solved different evaluations under different harnesses. ToolPrivacyBench found that none of nine evaluated agents consistently respected need-to-know disclosure boundaries across 2,150 cases, even when workflow completion was high. A consent-based mobility study re-identified 18 of 25 re-identifiable participants, while HORIZON drove controlled hardware benchmarks to their acceptance gates but explicitly warned that exposed feedback can produce harness-specific over-solving. Together, these sources support a reviewer inference: capability, privacy, and correctness must be measured over the same recorded trajectory.

The cooperation and scientific-method papers reinforce that structure improves tractability. LLawCo uses failure-derived laws as an editable interface for embodied cooperation. Quantum collision models require hardware-specific ancilla and compilation strategies. Latent SDE identification depends on distinct environmental diffusion ratios. Directed graph recovery combines filter identification with sparse structural constraints. FMO-xTB trades exact full-system computation for controlled fragment approximations and near-linear scaling. None proves a general theory of agent governance, but each shows a domain-specific way to make a complex process observable, constrained, and testable.

Reviewer confidence is medium-high in the source-specific results and medium in the cross-domain synthesis. All ten canonical records and relevant full-paper HTML sections were inspected, but no code, data, model, benchmark, proof, or numerical result was independently reproduced.

## Detailed Summary

### From conversational assistance to delegated production

S3 defines agentic work as delegated, multi-step production that can inspect context, use tools, and modify artifacts. Its strongest evidence is descriptive rather than causal. Codex adoption rose quickly, organizational use exceeded individual use, and intensive users increasingly combined long runtimes, concurrent turns, and reusable skills. The study also cautions through its design: OpenAI workers operate in a setting with unusually low adoption friction, high model familiarity, strong organizational support, and cheap marginal usage, so internal penetration should not be generalized to a typical organization.

The important shift is not merely more output. A delegated workflow introduces state transitions and verification obligations. The first broad request can fan out into tool calls, patches, documents, reviews, and follow-up turns. That makes turn-level or answer-level success an incomplete operational metric.

### Long-horizon capability belongs to the model–harness system

S4 operationalizes long-horizon scientific reasoning as recovering controlled biological claims from raw or near-raw single-cell data. Its 21 evaluations span multiple assays and biological contexts, with 1,068 completed trajectories in the benchmark matrix. The best pair passed 16 of 63 replicated runs. Common failures included substituting familiar biological priors for task-specific evidence, treating abundance as importance, confusing association with mechanism, and failing to integrate RNA and chromatin modalities.

Harness comparisons are especially relevant. Claude Opus 4.8 passed 16/63 with Claude Code versus 13/63 with PI; GPT-5.4 passed 2/63 with OpenAI Codex versus 6/63 with PI; GPT-5.5 reached 10/63 with both but solved different evaluations. The base model name therefore does not identify the evaluated system. Tool availability, context presentation, persistence, and execution policy materially change the result.

### Utility and privacy can diverge inside a successful workflow

S5 introduces a policy knowledge base linking private atoms to authorized tool purposes and information sinks. Mock backends record actual arguments, allowing the evaluator to compare each tool call with a purpose-bound authorization matrix. Across 1,150 synthetic business workflows and 1,000 adapted public benchmark cases, nine agents could execute many workflows but none consistently preserved need-to-know boundaries.

The failure mechanism is context collapse. Information legitimately acquired for an early clinical or payment step is copied into a later administrative summary or team handoff. A final answer can appear correct while an intermediate tool receives names, symptoms, financial details, or secret material it did not need. Privacy evaluation must therefore join utility metrics with field-, tool-, sink-, severity-, and path-level disclosure measures.

S6 shows a more direct downstream privacy risk. Using consent-based participants and simulated GPS traces anchored around real home and work addresses, an agentic pipeline searched public sources and attributed identities without ongoing human intervention. It identified 18 of 25 participants judged re-identifiable and 18 of 43 cases overall. The authors withheld code, prompts, skills, and traces because publication could facilitate misuse. The evidence is a bounded feasibility study, not a prevalence estimate, but it demonstrates that automation changes the cost side of the re-identification threat model.

### Executable feedback is powerful and dangerous

S7's HORIZON compiles a Markdown harness into domain knowledge, an evaluator, an acceptance predicate, and git/runtime policy. An agent works in an isolated repository, commits accepted attempts, records rejected attempts, and reuses session context. On ChipBench, RTLLM-2.0, Verilog-Eval, and nine CVDP categories, the reported run drove all suites to the configured pass condition. Some hard categories required dozens of iterations; CVDP CID 012 moved from a 47.8% first-iteration pass rate to 100% at iteration 32 while average parsed coverage reached 97.9%.

The authors explicitly delimit the claim. The acceptance gate was the visible benchmark harness, not exhaustive specification satisfaction. Detailed traces can help repair, but they can also invite customization to deterministic tests or evaluator quirks. Real chip design adds incomplete specifications, changing constraints, power/performance/area tradeoffs, integration, and independent validation. The generalizable result is that executable feedback supports convergence; the 100% figure is not evidence that autonomous hardware design is solved.

### Explicit laws as an interface for cooperation

S8 derives high-level cooperation laws from failed trajectories, uses them to select or reflect on traces, and aligns a backbone model through supervised fine-tuning. The resulting laws can also be edited by a human. On PARTNR-Dialog, LLawCo improved over communicative baselines across the reported backbones; for Qwen-3-14B it achieved 0.74 success versus 0.70 for CommPARTNR, while using fewer steps. The result needs nuance: a non-communicative ReAct baseline reached 0.62 success for LLaMA-3.1-8B versus LLawCo's 0.57, so “best” depends on which comparison class is used.

A controlled TDW-MAT intervention changed “send message” behavior from 55% without a law to 92% with LLawCo and 19% under an intervention. This provides evidence that explicit laws influence local policy, though it does not establish reliability outside the tested simulators. The design is relevant to governance because it exposes a compact, editable policy surface between raw failure traces and model behavior.

### Scientific systems: regimes, structure, hardware, and approximation

S9 implements Markovian open-system dynamics through quantum collision models on one trapped-ion and two superconducting platforms. Reset ancillas conserve qubits but add reset noise and circuit duration; fresh ancillas avoid reset noise but grow with time steps. Hardware-specific combinations and compilation enabled experiments up to seven system qubits, 13 qubits total, and 40 time steps. Post-selection improved some results but reduced effective samples and increased uncertainty.

S10 gives identifiability results for additive-noise latent SDEs observed through an unknown nonlinear map. Two diagonal diffusion regimes with distinct coordinate-wise variance ratios identify latent coordinates up to permutation and scaling; the drift-Jacobian graph follows under smoothness conditions. In synthetic experiments, distinct-ratio settings produced mean correlation near 0.998 and exact graph-match rates for sparse linear and nonlinear cases, whereas one-regime and proportional-diffusion controls were materially worse. The assumptions—especially an invertible observation map, shared drift, additive state-independent noise, and separated regimes—are strong and visible.

S11 studies directed topology inference when signals are generated by a polynomial graph filter. It first identifies the filter from second-order input/output statistics, then recovers a sparse admissible graph shift, with a joint refinement algorithm and convergence analysis. Synthetic performance remained near perfect on tested Erdős–Rényi and Barabási–Albert graphs but degraded with growing stochastic-block-model graphs. Uber mobility and stock-network examples demonstrate plausible use, not ground-truth confirmation of the inferred real graphs.

S12 combines fragment molecular orbitals with GFN1-xTB and analytic gradients. Reported scaling exponents ranged from 1.06 to 1.28 for fragmented calculations, compared with roughly cubic non-fragmented timings. A 23,760-atom pentacene system required about 63 seconds for parallel FMO2-xTB self-consistent charge computation and about 4.4 minutes for a complete FMO2 energy-and-gradient evaluation on the reported node. Approximation error grows in systems with stronger many-body polarization; FMO3 or larger fragments improve accuracy at additional cost.

## Observations

- `Observed pattern`: The evaluation target expands with autonomy. A response metric may be enough for a chatbot; an agent requires path-level records of tools, inputs, outputs, disclosure, retries, and acceptance.
- `Observed pattern`: Harnesses are active components. scBench-Long directly changes outcomes by harness, while HORIZON makes evaluator feedback and repository policy part of the mechanism.
- `Technical implication`: Versioned traces are useful only when paired with an independent acceptance surface. Reusing the same visible evaluator for iteration and final judgment invites over-solving.
- `Contradiction or tension`: More persistent context can improve repair and coordination, yet the same accumulated context raises privacy and context-collapse risk.
- `Contradiction or tension`: LLawCo's explicit laws improve communicative comparisons, but a simpler non-communicative baseline is competitive or better in some settings; additional machinery is not automatically superior.
- `Observed pattern`: Scientific methods gain identifiability or scalability by making structure explicit—diffusion regimes, graph priors, ancilla policy, or fragment boundaries.
- `Reviewer hypothesis`: Agent governance will benefit from treating policies as executable, versioned objects that can be checked at every transition rather than as prose attached only to the initial prompt.
- `Open question`: How much performance remains when the repair feedback is separated from a hidden final evaluator and when privacy views are regenerated at each tool boundary?

## Considerations

- Privacy policies need field- and purpose-level authorization, not a single “sensitive” flag. Free-text summaries are especially dangerous because they collapse earlier purpose-specific views.
- Benchmark owners should distinguish iterative repair performance, first-attempt performance, final hidden-test performance, and coverage. Combining them into one pass rate obscures reward-hacking risk.
- Scientific-agent evaluations should version both the base model and harness, record tool availability, preserve failed trajectories, and disclose deterministic grading logic where safe.
- Human-editable laws or policies can create accountability, but their induced behavior needs counterfactual tests; the existence of a readable rule does not prove compliance.
- Organizational adoption evidence should not be read as causal productivity evidence. Output tokens and task classifiers are proxies, and high-support environments may be atypical.
- Mobility re-identification research requires consent, data minimization, withholding of operational attack material, and review against applicable privacy law. The safe implementation target is detection and risk assessment.
- Hardware, quantum, graph, and chemistry results depend on domain-specific assumptions. A general agent layer must expose those assumptions rather than treating executable success as universal validity.
- Source licenses permit different forms of reuse. This artifact links and paraphrases; it does not redistribute source payloads.

## Three Ways to Exercise This Research

1. `Synthetic trajectory audit`: Objective—test whether a small tool-using workflow leaks fields across purposes. Inputs—a synthetic customer record, three mock tools, and a field-to-purpose matrix. Method—run the workflow, log each argument, and compare every disclosed field with the matrix. Output—a path-level utility/privacy report. Success criterion—task completion with zero unauthorized fields. Stop condition—halt if a real identifier or production credential appears.
2. `Paired harness replay`: Objective—measure harness effects on a bounded research task. Inputs—one public toy dataset, one model version, two versioned harnesses, and deterministic grading. Method—run at least three replicates per harness and compare both aggregate passes and which tasks switch outcome. Output—a model–harness comparison card. Success criterion—reproducible traces and explicit uncertainty. Stop condition—stop when cost or variance exceeds the predeclared budget.
3. `Hidden-gate repository repair`: Objective—separate iterative debugging from final acceptance. Inputs—a toy HDL or software repository, visible unit tests, and a withheld semantic test set controlled by the evaluator. Method—allow iterative repair on visible feedback, then perform one independent hidden evaluation. Output—a convergence trace and generalization gap. Success criterion—visible and hidden gates pass without policy violations. Stop condition—stop at the iteration cap or on any sandbox escape attempt.

## Example MVP Product

- `Product name`: Trajectory Gate
- `Target user`: Teams deploying tool-using agents in research, engineering, or regulated workflows.
- `Problem`: Final-answer review cannot reveal unauthorized intermediate disclosure, harness-specific over-solving, or unsupported domain assumptions.
- `Core workflow`: Import a versioned tool manifest and purpose policy; execute an agent in a sandbox; capture a redacted event trace; check every transition; run an independent final evaluator; produce a signed evidence card with exceptions.
- `Data requirements`: Synthetic or authorized tasks, tool schemas, field-purpose authorization matrix, evaluator definitions, redacted events, and version identifiers. Raw secrets and unnecessary personal data are prohibited.
- `Architecture`: Local or controlled execution runner; append-only event store; policy engine; disclosure detector; visible repair evaluator; separate hidden final evaluator; reviewer dashboard; exportable Markdown/JSON evidence card.
- `Success metrics`: Task completion, hidden-gate success, unauthorized-field rate, severity-weighted leakage, evaluator disagreement, reviewer override rate, and trace completeness.
- `Risk controls`: Least-privilege tools, per-sink context reconstruction, secret scanning, redaction before storage, retention limits, immutable policy versions, independent evaluator permissions, cost/iteration caps, and human escalation.
- `Limitations`: Policies can be incomplete, free-text detectors can miss semantic leakage, hidden tests can still be narrow, and trace collection may expose sensitive context if redaction fails.
- `MVP boundary`: No production personal data, autonomous external publication, offensive security actions, or claims of formal privacy guarantees.
- `Deployment model`: Local CLI and CI check over synthetic or pre-approved tasks.
- `Evaluation plan`: Unit tests for policy rules, synthetic ToolPrivacyBench-style cases, paired-harness replays, hidden-gate repository tasks, and privacy/security review.
- `Failure modes`: False blocks from over-restrictive policies, missed paraphrased disclosures, evaluator leakage into the repair loop, incomplete event capture, and misleading aggregate scores.
- `Maintenance plan`: Version policy schemas and evaluators; monitor detector drift; rotate hidden tasks; audit overrides; refresh source assumptions and domain cards.
