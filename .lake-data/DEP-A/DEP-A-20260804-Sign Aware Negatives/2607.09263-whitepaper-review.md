## 1. Executive Assessment

At a high level, the paper makes its memory decision surface explicit and evaluates that decision under named operational constraints.

The paper's strongest contribution is a concrete mechanism connected to an explicit evaluation, rather than a claim resting on the abstract alone. Experiments on PHOENIX-2014T demonstrate that SAN substantially improves fine-grained retrieval performance while preserving coarse-grained accuracy, outperforming language-model–based negative mining strategies. Experiments on PHOENIX-2014T demonstrate that SAN substantially improves fine-grained retrieval performance while preserving coarse-grained accuracy, highlighting the importance of aligning negative supervision with visual ambiguity in sign language retrieval. Specifically, applying SAN to CiCo boosts the fine-grained V2T R@1 from 17.9% to 39.4% , a remarkable absolute improvement of +21.5% . On the GFSLT-VLP backbone, our method achieves a V2T R@1 of 49.1% , surpassing the strongest text-based competitor, FastText (43.8%), by a clear margin. These are paper reports. No experiment, benchmark, training run, kernel measurement, or security test was independently rerun for this review.[^scope]

The source gate passed before drafting: the local PDF is 16 pages, begins and ends as a complete PDF, parses, contains the expected title and full body structure, and includes references; the matching full-paper HTML contains 61218 readable body characters, document structure, headings, and paper sections.[^integrity] The evidence is therefore suitable for a full-paper assessment rather than an abstract-level summary.

My bottom-line judgment is favorable but conditional. The method is technically legible and the reported comparisons establish a meaningful operating region. Our experiments are limited to the PHOENIX-2014T dataset within a single domain, and the generalizability of SAN across other sign languages and datasets remains to be validated. However, we believe that PHOENIX-2014T provides a particularly suitable testbed for studying fine-grained sign language retrieval, as its weather-forecast domain naturally exhibits a dense distribution of visually similar signs (e.g., regional names, numbers, and weather-related terms), creating a concentrated environment for evaluating visual confusability. Furthermore, although we employed POS-filtering to ensure grammatical consistency during fine-grained evaluation, some linguistic noise may still persist in the generated captions. The durable value is the mechanism and its testable boundary, not the largest number in isolation.

## 2. Problem Framing and Research Questions

The paper addresses a resource or state-management problem in which a naive representation becomes too expensive, stale, unsafe, or poorly aligned with a downstream task. Its research question is whether a structured decision can preserve the relevant outcome while reducing cost or strengthening an invariant. The right comparison is therefore a frontier: outcome versus compute, memory, bandwidth, latency, storage, or integrity under the same information boundary.

Five questions guide this review. First, what object is measured or transformed? Second, which decision is novel and which components are inherited? Third, what cost does the transformation add before the downstream consumer runs? Fourth, under which datasets, models, traces, budgets, and hardware does the reported advantage hold? Fifth, what observation would falsify the proposed explanation rather than merely lower an aggregate score?

The paper's framing is useful because it makes one previously implicit control surface explicit. That does not mean the chosen proxy is identical to user value. A cache error may or may not alter an answer; a memory hit may or may not be used; a smaller representation may or may not reduce wall time; authenticated latent state may still contain false reasoning. The review therefore keeps mechanism, proxy, task result, and deployment objective separate.

## 4. Experimental Design and Evidence Reconstructed

Beyond standard retrieval accuracy, our goal is to evaluate a model’s ability to resolve visually confusable signs , which frequently arise in sign language due to its constrained articulatory space, yet are not explicitly captured by existing benchmarks. In particular, dictionary-level phonological features can be used to identify minimal pairs —sign pairs differing in only a single phonological component—which serve as a proxy for visual hardness without requiring visual data. These results indicate that SAN effectively mines hard negatives from visually confusable regions, covering both semantically similar and semantically distinct cases that are often missed by language-model-based methods.
