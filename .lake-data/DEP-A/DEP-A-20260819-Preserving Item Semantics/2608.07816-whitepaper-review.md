The paper addresses a resource or state-management problem in which a naive representation becomes too expensive, stale, unsafe, or poorly aligned with a downstream task. Its research question is whether a structured decision can preserve the relevant outcome while reducing cost or strengthening an invariant. The right comparison is therefore a frontier: outcome versus compute, memory, bandwidth, latency, storage, or integrity under the same information boundary.

Five questions guide this review. First, what object is measured or transformed? Second, which decision is novel and which components are inherited? Third, what cost does the transformation add before the downstream consumer runs? Fourth, under which datasets, models, traces, budgets, and hardware does the reported advantage hold? Fifth, what observation would falsify the proposed explanation rather than merely lower an aggregate score?

The paper's framing is useful because it makes one previously implicit control surface explicit. That does not mean the chosen proxy is identical to user value. ## 4. Experimental Design and Evidence Reconstructed

Consistent with the recall improvements observed in the main text, centroid initialization consistently outperforms the random baseline across all datasets for both NDCG@5 and NDCG@10. For example, on the Toys and Sports datasets, centroid initialization yields relative NDCG@5 improvements of 11.1% and 9.5%, respectively, showing that preserving the underlying semantic geometry translates directly into a higher-quality ranking distribution. For example, on the Toys, Steam, and MovieLens datasets, the centroid-initialized models achieve strong NDCG and Recall@10 metrics with just 1 to 4 CPT epochs, whereas the random baseline tends to require additional epochs to reach similar performance. Ultimately, these metrics corroborate our earlier findings that centroid initialization provides strong tail-item generalization, largely bypassing the need for costly CPT epochs to establish cold-item semantics.

Resource accounting must follow the whole path. A smaller stored object is not automatically lower latency; a lower arithmetic count is not automatically higher throughput; and an average gain does not determine tail behavior.

Baseline fairness requires the same information boundary, comparable tuning, matched model revision, compatible budget, and competent implementation. If one method receives future-query information, a recent-token buffer, extra calibration data, or a more favorable kernel, the comparison must say so. The closest practical baseline and an intentionally simple baseline serve different diagnostic purposes and both are useful.

Metric semantics are part of the claim. In this section, we directly address the relationship between our token-level diagnostics and item-level behavioral popularity bias. Specifically, our analyses evaluate popularity through two granularities: item-level popularity f i f_{i} and token-level popularity n k = ∑ i ∈ ℐ k f i n_{k}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}f_{i} , where ℐ k = { i : k 0 ​ ( i ) = k } \mathcal{I}_{k}=\{i:k_{0}(i)=k\} is the set of items assigned to level-0 code k k . While token-level popularity is derived from item popularity, we note that relying on a single codebook level introduces ambiguity because aggregate frequency conceals the distribution of constituent items within a code. Mathematically, level-0 code frequency factors into cluster size and average item popularity ( n k = | ℐ k | ⋅ f ¯ k n_{k}=|\mathcal{I}_{k}|\cdot\bar{f}_{k} ). Figure 9 illustrates how these underlying factors behave across datasets. For instance, on Steam and MovieLens, average item popularity spreads widely, meaning a level-0 code’s frequency strongly reflects the popularity of its assigned items. Conversely, on the Amazon domains, average item popularity is more concentrated, meaning variance in level-0 code frequency is a mixture of cluster size and actual item popularity.
